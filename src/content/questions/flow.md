When a piece of work takes six weeks to reach a user, it is tempting to look for the person or team who was slow. Follow the work closely, though, and a different picture tends to appear. A few days of genuine effort are scattered through those six weeks. The rest is waiting: for a ticket to be prioritised, for a dependency to be released, for a review, for an environment, for an approval, for the next planning cycle to come round.

Activity and flow are different measures. Activity asks whether people are busy. Utilisation asks how fully their time is booked. Flow asks how quickly and how predictably value moves from a request to an outcome. An organisation can be very busy, very fully utilised, and very slow all at the same time. In fact high utilisation is one of the most reliable ways to create queues, because a fully booked team has no spare capacity to absorb the next request without making something else wait.

The waits are easy to miss because nobody owns them. A handoff between two teams belongs to neither. An approval gate looks like diligence from the inside. A dependency on a shared platform team is simply "how things are done here". Each one is locally reasonable. Together they can account for most of the elapsed time, and they rarely show up on anyone's dashboard.

## What to look for

Queues form wherever work arrives faster than it can be taken on. Dependencies create waits whenever one team needs something from another before it can continue. Handoffs lose context as well as time, so the work often comes back with questions. Approval gates batch decisions into meetings that happen on a calendar rather than when the work is ready. Coordination delays appear whenever several people need to agree on the same thing at the same time.

None of these is a sign of bad people. They are properties of the structure the work has to pass through, which means they can be changed by changing the structure rather than by asking individuals to try harder.

Noticing where work waits is the first step. Once you can see the waits, you can start asking why the structure produces them, and which ones are worth the effort to shorten.
